# C. Exercise - Train hard enough on purpose

Once [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]] has earned you a base, this is the activity pillar: progressive strength and conditioning for people who still live under demand.

## Progressive resistance still works

The [National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA)](https://www.nsca.com) position statement on resistance training for older adults is the programming spine here: progressive plans, meaningful intensity, multi-joint lifts, and power work at moderate loads<sup>[[G-Sources/S04-fragala-nsca-older-adults|S04]]</sup> ([NSCA statement](https://www.nsca.com/about-us/position-statements/resistance-training-for-older-adults/)). My view: hard training after midlife is not vanity. For this audience, it is how you keep capacity for the mat, the uniform, and the job.

Dose still matters. Frequency, intensity, and volume ranges for healthy older adults are mapped in [Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher) and colleagues<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup>. My advice: use that evidence to audit social media templates before they outrank your recovery. [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]] is where volume honesty meets the plan.

## Strength and bone stay trainable

Progressive resistance training can increase lower-limb strength and support femur and hip bone mineral density in older adults. That is the combined message of [O’Bryan](https://researchers.vu.edu.au/3351-steven-o'bryan) and colleagues’ systematic review<sup>[[G-Sources/S33-obryan-bone-strength|S33]]</sup> ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01675-2)). Pair it with the anabolic-resistance warning from [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]] and [Endo](https://connects.catalyst.harvard.edu/Profiles/display/Person/165349)<sup>[[G-Sources/S32-endo-anabolic-aging|S32]]</sup>. My synthesis of those two lines: the stimulus may buy less, so the plan must be smarter, not softer by default.

## Concurrent work must be designed

If you lift and also run, ruck, or train martial arts, interference is real. Concurrent strength and endurance work in midlife and older adults needs session design and sequencing, not “just add conditioning” ([Markov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Markov+A%5BAuthor%5D+Chaabene+concurrent))<sup>[[G-Sources/S03-markov-concurrent|S03]]</sup> ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01764-2)). When volume piles up, [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]] is the check before another hard day.

## Lived practice fills gaps science rarely speaks to

Scholarship samples “healthy older adults.” Lived practice speaks from the mat. [Christensen](https://www.lwcbooks.com/)’s *Solo Training 3: 50 and Older* is written by someone who kept training and teaching past midlife<sup>[[G-Sources/S09-christensen-solo-training-50|S09]]</sup> ([book](https://www.amazon.com/Solo-Training-Loren-W-Christensen/dp/1517332400)). Check hard claims against [Fragala](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=TtMk4mUAAAAJ&hl=en)<sup>[[G-Sources/S04-fragala-nsca-older-adults|S04]]</sup> and [Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher)<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup>. Joint-friendly conditioning with athletic intent sits beside that lane in [Ross](https://ymaa.com/) / *Tai Chi Fit Over 60*<sup>[[G-Sources/S10-ross-tai-chi-fit-over-60|S10]]</sup>.

Still-training research ([McKendry](https://news.ubc.ca/expert/dr-james-mckendry/)<sup>[[G-Sources/S01-mckendry-master-athletes|S01]]</sup>) reminds you that chronically trained bodies are not sedentary samples. Use both science and practice.
