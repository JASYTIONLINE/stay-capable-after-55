# C. Exercise - Train hard enough on purpose

[Loren W. Christensen](https://www.lwcbooks.com/) has trained and taught martial arts since 1965. He holds an 8th dan in American Free Style Karate, ranks in jujitsu and Modern Arnis, and spent years as a law-enforcement officer before writing dozens of books for fighters who refuse to quit. In *Solo Training 3: 50 and Older*<sup>[[G-Sources/S09-christensen-solo-training-50|S09]]</sup> ([book](https://www.amazon.com/Solo-Training-Loren-W-Christensen/dp/1517332400)), he is blunt: this is not about keeping up with twenty-year-olds. It is about keeping speed, power, flexibility, and fight skill for years more by changing the week, not abandoning the craft.

His own template after midlife is a standard routine, not a mood-based scramble. Martial arts days. Weight days. A shorter solo session. A rest day or light shadowboxing. He says nothing about the plan is magic except that he does it consistently, and that by accepting a modified schedule he held onto most of what mattered instead of losing it by chasing the old self.

Once [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]] has earned you a base, this page is the activity pillar: train hard enough on purpose, on a week you can repeat. After midlife, intermittent bursts and long disappearing acts cost more than they used to. The requirement is a standard routine with planned easier days. When you are tired or worn down, lower the intensity and stay in the habit. Do not take extended time off and recreate the comeback problem [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]] already warned you about.

## Keep a week you can repeat

Younger athletes often get away with training hard when life allows and vanishing for weeks when it does not. After 55, capacity falls faster when the habit breaks, and the return gets riskier. Planned rest and lighter sessions belong inside the plan. Multi-week dropout does not.

Christensen’s week is one lived model of that idea: hard enough to progress, soft enough to last, and scheduled so recovery is intentional. Masters coaching names the same trap from the other side. Many people still under the bar are volume-sensitive; “just add more” often produces stalls and soreness instead of progress ([Sullivan](https://startingstrength.com/guest/jonathon-sullivan) and [Baker](https://www.andybaker.com/))<sup>[[G-Sources/S07-sullivan-masters-volume|S07]]</sup>. [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]] is where that honesty meets the calendar.

## Progressive resistance still works

Hard training after midlife is not vanity. It is how you keep capacity for the mat, the uniform, and the job. The [National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA)](https://www.nsca.com) position statement on resistance training for older adults is the programming spine here: progressive plans, meaningful intensity, multi-joint lifts, and power work at moderate loads<sup>[[G-Sources/S04-fragala-nsca-older-adults|S04]]</sup> ([NSCA statement](https://www.nsca.com/about-us/position-statements/resistance-training-for-older-adults/)). Lead author [Maren Fragala](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=TtMk4mUAAAAJ&hl=en) and colleagues write for people who still need strength, not for a sedentary “senior stretch” class.

Dose still matters. Frequency, intensity, and volume ranges for healthy older adults are mapped by [Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher) and colleagues<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup>. Use that evidence to audit social-media templates before they outrank your recovery.

Aging can blunt the return on the same stimulus. [Endo](https://connects.catalyst.harvard.edu/Profiles/display/Person/165349) and [Nourmahnad](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Nourmahnad+A) explain why the same lift can buy less muscle after midlife<sup>[[G-Sources/S32-endo-anabolic-aging|S32]]</sup> ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00874)). Progressive resistance can still increase lower-limb strength and support femur and hip bone mineral density ([O’Bryan](https://researchers.vu.edu.au/3351-steven-o'bryan) and colleagues)<sup>[[G-Sources/S33-obryan-bone-strength|S33]]</sup> ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01675-2)). The plan must be smarter, not softer by default, and consistent enough that the stimulus keeps arriving.

Still-training research ([McKendry](https://news.ubc.ca/expert/dr-james-mckendry/)<sup>[[G-Sources/S01-mckendry-master-athletes|S01]]</sup>) reminds you that chronically trained bodies are not sedentary samples. You are not writing a retirement fitness pamphlet. You are recalibrating a demanding life.

## Concurrent work must be designed

If you lift,  run, ruck, or train martial arts, interference is real. Concurrent strength and endurance work in midlife and older adults needs session design and sequencing, not “just add conditioning” ([Markov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Markov+A%5BAuthor%5D+Chaabene+concurrent))<sup>[[G-Sources/S03-markov-concurrent|S03]]</sup> ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01764-2)). Christensen’s split of mat days and weight days is one practical answer to that problem. When volume piles up, check [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]] before you stack another hard day.

## When you are worn down, downshift. Do not disappear

Stopping altogether when you feel cooked is how intermittent training sneaks back in. Lower the intensity instead. Shorter solo work. Technique. Mobility. Joint-friendly conditioning with athletic intent, the lane [David-Dorian Ross](https://ymaa.com/) maps for adults past sixty in *Tai Chi Fit Over 60*<sup>[[G-Sources/S10-ross-tai-chi-fit-over-60|S10]]</sup>. Easy shadowboxing. A walk with purpose. The point is to stay active and on point while tissue and nervous system catch up.

That is different from a long layoff. Planned easier days protect the habit. Extended time off returns you to [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]] with an unearned base and a mind that still remembers last year’s pace.

Christensen’s hard programming claims are lived practice, not peer-reviewed trials. Check them against [Fragala](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=TtMk4mUAAAAJ&hl=en)<sup>[[G-Sources/S04-fragala-nsca-older-adults|S04]]</sup> and [Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher)<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup> before you copy intensity you have not earned.

## What to do

1. **Write a standard week you can keep.** Put hard days, skill days, and planned easier days on the calendar before life decides for you. If your plan only works when motivation is high, it is not a plan after midlife.

2. **Progress strength on purpose.** Use multi-joint lifts, meaningful intensity, and a progression you can track. Audit frequency, load, and volume against recovery instead of copying your forties or a random online template ([Fragala](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=TtMk4mUAAAAJ&hl=en) / NSCA<sup>[[G-Sources/S04-fragala-nsca-older-adults|S04]]</sup> · [Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher)<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup>).

3. **Design concurrent work.** Separate or sequence lifting, running, rucking, and mat work so one does not quietly erase the other ([Markov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Markov+A%5BAuthor%5D+Chaabene+concurrent)<sup>[[G-Sources/S03-markov-concurrent|S03]]</sup>). Do not “just add conditioning” on top of a full strength week.

4. **Downshift when worn out; do not take extended time off.** Replace a hard session with lower-intensity training that keeps you moving and sharp. Save long layoffs for real medical need. Otherwise you pay for the gap in [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]].
