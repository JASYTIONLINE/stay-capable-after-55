# E. Sleep and recovery - Protect the work

Sleep and recovery close the triad loop from [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]]. After [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] and [[D-Nutrition/index|Nutrition]] build the sessions, this is what keeps them from getting erased.

## Sleep is capacity, not optional wellness

Athlete sleep guidance from [Walsh](https://profiles.ljmu.ac.uk/14927-neil-walsh) and colleagues in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* treats short or poor sleep as a performance and recovery risk, with screening, education, and a toolbox of individual targets rather than one magic hour rule<sup>[[G-Sources/S13-walsh-athlete-sleep|S13]]</sup> ([BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/7/356)). That matches [FM 7-22](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN44522-FM_7-22-002-WEB-7.pdf)<sup>[[G-Sources/S15-fm-7-22-h2f|S15]]</sup>: sleep is a readiness behavior. My conclusion for this audience: under-sleep after midlife is not a badge of toughness. It is how hard weeks fail.

## Volume honesty for masters athletes

Many masters lifters are volume-sensitive. Adding sets and frequency often produces soreness and stalls rather than progress. [Sullivan](https://startingstrength.com/guest/jonathon-sullivan) and [Baker](https://www.andybaker.com/) name that failure mode for people still under the bar<sup>[[G-Sources/S07-sullivan-masters-volume|S07]]</sup> ([Starting Strength](https://startingstrength.com/article/volume-and-the-masters-lifter) · [PDF](https://startingstrength.com/contentfiles/volume_and_the_masters_lifter.pdf)). Triangulate with dose evidence ([Borde](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Borde+R%5BAuthor%5D+Granacher)<sup>[[G-Sources/S02-borde-dose-response|S02]]</sup>) and concurrent-design ([Markov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Markov+A%5BAuthor%5D+Chaabene+concurrent)<sup>[[G-Sources/S03-markov-concurrent|S03]]</sup>) before you “just add more.” The same honesty protects the comeback rules in [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]].

## Recovery is part of the job

[ACSM](https://www.acsm.org) recovery guidance for active older adults covers sleep, nutrition, hydration, and active recovery on hard weeks<sup>[[G-Sources/S21-acsm-recovery-older-adults|S21]]</sup> ([ACSM page](https://acsm.org/recovery-active-older-adults/)). Use it as a checklist, not a product aisle. When recovery products promise miracles, weigh them against [Walsh](https://profiles.ljmu.ac.uk/14927-neil-walsh) and ACSM, not marketing.

My synthesis: longer recovery needs after midlife are a major reason the old mid-40s week often fails for people like me. Physiology ([Endo](https://connects.catalyst.harvard.edu/Profiles/display/Person/165349)<sup>[[G-Sources/S32-endo-anabolic-aging|S32]]</sup>), comeback risk ([Willich](https://epidemiologie.charite.de/en/metas/person/person/address_detail/prof_dr_med_stefan_n_willich_mph_mba)<sup>[[G-Sources/S31-willich-exertion-mi-trigger|S31]]</sup>), and volume honesty ([Sullivan](https://startingstrength.com/guest/jonathon-sullivan)<sup>[[G-Sources/S07-sullivan-masters-volume|S07]]</sup>) support pieces of that picture. My bottom line: protect the work.
