# E. Sleep and recovery - Protect the work

After 55 I sleep less than I used to. I wake around 6 a.m. with or without an alarm, and I often do not get to bed until after midnight. Eight hours feels impossible. The shortfall shows up the next day on the mat, under the bar, and in how sharp I am at work. Hard training without recovery is not toughness. It is unpaid debt.

In [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]] and Army [Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F)](https://www.army.mil/article/239475/holistic_health_added_to_army_fitness_doctrine) doctrine in [Field Manual (FM) 7-22](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN44522-FM_7-22-002-WEB-7.pdf)<sup>[[G-Sources/S15-fm-7-22-h2f|S15]]</sup>, sleep, activity, and nutrition are one readiness system. [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] builds the sessions. [[D-Nutrition/index|Nutrition]] fuels them. Sleep is what lets the body and the brain keep the gains. Athlete guidance from [Walsh](https://profiles.ljmu.ac.uk/14927-neil-walsh) and colleagues treats short or poor sleep as a performance and recovery risk, with screening and individual targets rather than one magic hour rule<sup>[[G-Sources/S13-walsh-athlete-sleep|S13]]</sup> ([BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/7/356)). [ACSM](https://www.acsm.org) puts sleep beside nutrition and load for active older adults<sup>[[G-Sources/S21-acsm-recovery-older-adults|S21]]</sup> ([ACSM page](https://acsm.org/recovery-active-older-adults/)).

## Why sleep is recovery work

Sleep is when tissue repair, hormone balance, immune function, memory consolidation, and emotional regulation get room to finish the job. Cut it short and you do not only feel tired. Strength sessions feel flatter. Skill and judgment get noisier. Mood and stress tolerance drop ([Palmer and Alfano](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8193556/)). Motivation to keep a standard week in [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] falls with them. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html) frames healthy sleep as enough sleep *and* good quality sleep: uninterrupted enough that you wake restored, not only “in bed for seven hours.” For athletes, Walsh’s consensus is blunt: treat sleep like part of the training plan, not optional wellness<sup>[[G-Sources/S13-walsh-athlete-sleep|S13]]</sup>.

Quality matters as much as clock time. You want mostly continuous sleep, enough deep and REM stages over the night, and mornings that do not feel like a hangover. Broken nights, loud snoring with gasping, or waking exhausted after a “full” night are signals that quantity alone is not fixing the problem. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI)](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation) covers what sleep deprivation costs across body and brain when you want the deeper clinical picture.

## How sleep changes after midlife

Older adults still need roughly the same total sleep as younger adults ([National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/sleep/sleep-and-older-adults)), but the night often changes shape. Many people go to bed earlier, wake earlier, spend less time in deep sleep, wake more often, and find sleep lighter and easier to shatter. Medications, pain, and medical conditions pile on. NIA’s [Sleep and Older Adults](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/sleep/sleep-and-older-adults) page walks through those age-related shifts in plain language.

That is the lived pattern behind my late nights and early mornings. The calendar still demands strength. The night no longer forgives the same habits. When recovery windows shrink, the old mid-40s week fails for a simple reason: the work still arrives, the tear down occurs, but the rebuild does not.

## When sleep itself is the problem: insomnia and apnea

Not every bad night is “poor discipline.”

**Insomnia** means regular trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or both, with daytime cost. Short-term insomnia often follows stress or schedule shock. Chronic insomnia lasts months and needs a real plan, not another bottle from the checkout aisle. The [American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM)](https://aasm.org) backs behavioral care first for chronic insomnia, especially cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), before leaning on medications ([AASM behavioral therapies review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/)).

**Obstructive sleep apnea** is repeated airway collapse during sleep. Breathing pauses, snoring, gasping, and daytime sleepiness are common flags. Untreated apnea wrecks sleep quality and raises cardiovascular risk. Alcohol makes airway collapse worse. The [NHLBI sleep apnea overview](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea) and NIA’s older-adult sleep page are solid starting points; diagnosis and treatment (CPAP, oral devices, position, or other care) belong with a clinician, not a podcast.

If nights are chronically broken, loud, or exhausting, get evaluated. No amount of “sleep hygiene” fixes an airway that keeps closing.

## Pitfalls that feel like help and act like sabotage

A lot of midlife athletes reach for a nightcap, a melatonin gummy, or an over-the-counter sleep aid when the week is hard. The short-term story and the overnight story often disagree. High-demand operators fall into the same trap at scale: chronic sleep debt, alcohol to come down, then prescription sleep aids to force the lights out, then mixing the two.

For those suffering from difficulties falling asleep, like those with insomnia or PTSD, **alcohol** can make you drowsy and shorten the time to fall asleep. Unfortunately, the realities is then it fragments the night, suppresses REM, increases awakenings in the second half of sleep, and worsens breathing problems. NIA is direct: avoid alcohol before bed, even in small amounts ([NIA](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/sleep/sleep-and-older-adults)). The [NIAAA Alcohol Alert on alcohol and sleep](https://pubs.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/aa41.htm) goes deeper on architecture and apnea risk. In special operations communities, [Frueh and colleagues](https://chrisfrueh.com/docs/operator-syndrome-research-paper-Frueh-et-al-2020-2.pdf) describe sleep disturbance as nearly universal and alcohol as a common way to cope with pain, hypervigilance, and insomnia. That “help” further wrecks sleep architecture and mood.

**Melatonin** is a timing signal, not a knockout pill. Timed melatonin can help some circadian problems such as jet lag. For ordinary chronic insomnia in adults, AASM cautions against treating melatonin like a default fix ([AASM on melatonin](https://aasm.org/missing-the-mark-melatonin-finding-best-treatment-insomnia/)). Dose, purity, and timing vary wildly in supplements. Read the [NIH ODS melatonin fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Melatonin-Consumer/) before you stack it with other sedatives, and talk with your clinician if you use it regularly.

**OTC and prescription sleep aids** can leave next-day fog, dependence risk, and interactions with other medications. After midlife, clearance and side-effect risk change. Former Navy SEAL physician [Kirk Parsley](https://docparsley.com/) has described how operators stacked Ambien-class drugs with cocktails and destroyed the sleep stages that actually recover the body and brain ([Ready State conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2urgViAk9-E)). The [VA zolpidem guidance](https://www.veteranshealthlibrary.va.gov/medicationsva/203,a693025) is blunt: do not drink alcohol with these medicines; alcohol makes the side effects worse. Mixing sedating sleep products with alcohol raises the risk of amnesia, complex sleep behaviors, hallucinations, and other neuropsychiatric reactions that feel nothing like recovery. Do not build a nightly chemistry experiment. If insomnia is chronic, ask about CBT-I and medical causes first. AASM’s pharmacologic guideline is the deep read when a clinician is weighing medication ([AASM pharmacologic insomnia guideline](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.6470)).

The pattern is the same across these shortcuts: something that helps you *start* sleep can still wreck the *quality* of sleep your training needs, and stacking alcohol with sleep aids can turn a bad night into a dangerous one. Flat sessions the next day are not a mystery when the night was chemically broken.

If you have difficulty getting to sleep, most short-term fixes tend to create long-term problems. Nightcaps, melatonin stacks, and sedating pills can mask the issue for a week and teach the body the wrong lesson for a year. Rebuild the habit instead. [Mollie Eastman’s](https://www.sleepisaskill.com/about) podcast [*Sleep Is a Skill*](https://www.sleepisaskill.com/podcasts) is a practical place to learn how to change sleep habits in a healthy, long-term way: light, timing, behavior, and accountability rather than another chemical shortcut. Pair that kind of skill work with clinical paths like CBT-I when insomnia is chronic ([AASM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/)).

## Levers that work together

Good sleep after midlife is rarely one trick. It is a stack of small, consistent signals that tell the nervous system night has started, while training load and food stop fighting the night.

**Light and schedule.** Bright light early in the day and dimmer evenings help set the body clock. Screens and overhead glare late at night pull the other way. Keep a consistent bedtime and wake time when life allows. CDC’s sleep basics cover the core hygiene list ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); NHLBI explains how light hits the clock ([NHLBI sleep deprivation / sleep basics](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation)).

**Wind-down for the mind.** Worry, unfinished work, and fight-or-flight leftovers keep sleep shallow. A short wind-down (quiet reading, breath work, or meditation) lowers arousal so sleep can take. NCCIH summarizes what meditation and mindfulness evidence can and cannot claim ([NCCIH meditation overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-what-you-need-to-know)). For chronic insomnia, CBT-I remains the first-line behavioral path ([AASM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/)).

**Sound and rhythm tools.** Some people use steady sound, white noise, or binaural-beat audio as a wind-down cue. Binaural beats are not magic music: when each ear hears a slightly different pure tone, the brain can perceive a third “beat” at the difference frequency ([Chaieb et al. review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4428073/)). Marketers often claim that beat “entrains” your brainwaves into deep sleep. A systematic review of EEG studies finds that evidence for reliable brainwave entrainment is inconsistent at best ([Ingendoh, Posny, and Heine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10198548/)). Treat these as optional aids that may help you relax, not as proven replacements for treating apnea or insomnia. If you experiment, use headphones as intended, keep volume low, and drop anything that leaves you wired.

**Pre-sleep food and stimulants.** Heavy late meals, late caffeine, and nicotine all fight sleep. A modest evening meal that fits your [[D-Nutrition/index|Nutrition]] plan is different from a feast at 10 p.m. When you train hard, a measured pre-sleep protein feeding can support overnight recovery without turning into a second dinner<sup>[[G-Sources/S14-trommelen-presleep-protein|S14]]</sup>. Align carbs with the training day earlier; do not use alcohol as the “carb” that puts you under.

**Training load honesty.** Sleep debt and ego volume amplify each other. Many masters athletes are volume-sensitive; adding sets when recovery is already behind produces stalls and soreness instead of progress ([Sullivan](https://startingstrength.com/guest/jonathon-sullivan) and [Baker](https://www.andybaker.com/))<sup>[[G-Sources/S07-sullivan-masters-volume|S07]]</sup>. When nights are thin, downshift intensity in [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] instead of disappearing for two weeks and recreating the comeback problem in [[B-Returning-to-active-lifestyle/index|Returning]].

None of these levers cancel a sleep disorder. They stack best when breathing is clear, caffeine and alcohol are honest, light and schedule are roughly consistent, the evening meal fits the day, and the training week respects what the night can repay.

## Decide and act

Protect sleep like you protect a hard session. Aim for enough hours *and* sleep you can stay in. Get loud snoring, gasping, or chronic insomnia evaluated. Do not use alcohol, melatonin stacks, or mixed sedatives as the plan. Build a repeatable wind-down: light control, a calm mind, food that supports overnight recovery without overload, and a training week that matches recovery<sup>[[G-Sources/S13-walsh-athlete-sleep|S13]]</sup> · <sup>[[G-Sources/S21-acsm-recovery-older-adults|S21]]</sup>.

I still want better evidence on how sleep duration typically shortens after 55 under occupational demand, and how fragmented nights interact with masters training week to week. That open question lives in [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]]. Until sharper answers arrive, treat sleep as capacity equipment: train with purpose in [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]], eat for the work in [[D-Nutrition/index|Nutrition]], and give the night a real chance to finish the job.
