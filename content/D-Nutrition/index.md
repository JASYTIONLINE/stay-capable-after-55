# D. Nutrition - Fuel for the work

Lately, the way I eat stopped matching the way I train. The same meals that worked in my forties started stacking weight easier, holding water longer, and nudging blood pressure in the wrong direction. Keto and Paleo gave me temporary wins I could not keep. I still had a job, a mat, and a bar that demanded strength. I did not need another diet identity. I needed food that fueled the work, protected muscle, and lasted for years.

In [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]] and Army [Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F)](https://www.army.mil/article/239475/holistic_health_added_to_army_fitness_doctrine) doctrine in [Field Manual (FM) 7-22](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN44522-FM_7-22-002-WEB-7.pdf)<sup>[[G-Sources/S15-fm-7-22-h2f|S15]]</sup>, sleep, activity, and nutrition are one readiness system. [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] is how you train. Food is how you feed that training. No single meal plan fits every body. Use a clear process, a few solid numbers, and trusted tools to build a plan that fits *your* life.

[Barbara Strasser](https://www.sfu.ac.at/en/person/pruller-strasser-barbara/) and her colleagues, writing for older athletes who still train, put it simply: you need enough energy and enough protein to protect muscle and bone, plus honest attention to vitamins and minerals<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup> ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1409)).

## First, decide what you want food to do

Before you count a single gram, decide the job.

If you want to **lose fat**, you are asking food to help you drop fat while you keep the muscle, strength, and capacity your job and your mat still require. That means eating a little less than you burn, not starving yourself into weakness.

If you want to **gain muscle**, you are asking food to support hard training with a little extra fuel so the body has something to build with. That means eating a little more than you burn, while you keep progressive work in [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]].

If you want to **maintain**, you are asking food to hold your weight and physique steady while you still train hard on some days and easier on others. That means eating near what you burn, and moving carbohydrate up or down with the training week.

After midlife, protect muscle first. Protein stays high for all three goals. What mostly changes is total calories and how much carbohydrate you put around hard sessions. The [ISSN diets and body composition position stand](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)<sup>[[G-Sources/S34-issn-diets-body-composition|S34]]</sup> covers how surplus, deficit, and protein shape the body.

If blood pressure, medications, kidney disease, or other medical issues are part of your picture, build this with your doctor or a registered dietitian, not from a blog alone. You can find credentialed help through the [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics](https://www.eatright.org/find-a-nutrition-expert).

## Then find your calorie ballpark
(not medical advice-talk to your physician or nutritionist to verify your personal needs)

Think of calories as how much fuel the day costs. Sex, body weight, height, age, and activity all change that number. A desk job plus one easy walk is not the same burn as Guard drill, mat nights, and lifting. Calculators give you a starting estimate, not a courtroom verdict. When the stakes are high, a clinician’s assessment beats any app.

A common starting path is to estimate how much you burn at rest (the [Mifflin–St Jeor](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/) approach is one well-known method<sup>[[G-Sources/S35-mifflin-st-jeor|S35]]</sup>), then multiply by how active you actually are. Do that math with a reputable planner:

- [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp)
- [USDA DRI Calculator for Healthcare Professionals](https://www.nal.usda.gov/human-nutrition-and-food-science/dri-calculator)
- [NIH ODS Nutrient Recommendations and Databases](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx)<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup>

Once you have a maintenance estimate, steer it toward your goal. Hold steady near that number. To lose fat, come down a few hundred calories, not off a cliff. To gain muscle, go up a little. Then watch the scale, the waist, your strength, and how you feel for two or three weeks, and adjust. More detail sits in the [ISSN body-composition stand](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)<sup>[[G-Sources/S34-issn-diets-body-composition|S34]]</sup>.

Someone around 176 pounds who trains moderately might land near the mid-2000s for maintenance on the [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp). A cut might sit a few hundred lower. A lean-gain block might sit a few hundred higher. Your number will be different. Put *your* numbers into the [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp) or the [USDA DRI Calculator](https://www.nal.usda.gov/human-nutrition-and-food-science/dri-calculator), then let the scale and [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] tell you if you guessed right.

## Build the fuel mix: protein first, then the rest

Protein, carbohydrate, and fat are the three big fuels. Protein and carbs give you about four calories per gram. Fat gives you about nine. After midlife, start with protein, because the same training stimulus often buys less muscle than it used to ([Endo](https://connects.catalyst.harvard.edu/Profiles/display/Person/165349) and [Nourmahnad](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Nourmahnad+A))<sup>[[G-Sources/S32-endo-anabolic-aging|S32]]</sup>. That is why people who still train need more protein than the old sedentary chart of 0.8 grams per kilogram.

If you still train and you want to hold your strength, a solid everyday target is about **1.6 grams of protein for every kilogram you weigh**. For someone around 176 pounds (80 kilograms), that is roughly **128 grams a day**. Spread it across the day so each meal carries a real serving, often in the ballpark of a palm-sized portion or about 35 to 40 grams when you are aiming at that daily total<sup>[[G-Sources/S28-issn-protein-stand|S28]]</sup> · <sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup>. The research supports a wider band for exercising adults; start near 1.6 and adjust. Read the details in the [ISSN protein stand](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)<sup>[[G-Sources/S28-issn-protein-stand|S28]]</sup> and in [Strasser’s older-athlete review](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/5/1409)<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup>.

If you are cutting calories to lose fat while you keep lifting, raise protein further. That is how you tell the body to burn fat instead of the muscle you still need for the job and the mat<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup> · <sup>[[G-Sources/S28-issn-protein-stand|S28]]</sup>.

Next, keep enough fat in the plan that meals feel satisfying and the diet stays livable. Then fill what is left with carbohydrate to power [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]]. Hard days get more carbs. Easy or downshift days get less. A lot of older athletes under-eat carbohydrate around training and wonder why the sessions feel flat; coach [Ric Stern](https://www.cyclecoach.com/) has written about that same pattern in masters cyclists ([Road Bike Rider](https://www.roadbikerider.com/masters-cyclists-under-fuelling/))<sup>[[G-Sources/S37-stern-masters-underfuelling|S37]]</sup>. When the work earns it, post-session carbohydrate in the neighborhood of about 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram shows up again and again in that literature<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup>. Do not worship a magic percent split like “40/40/20.” Build from your goal calories, lock protein, keep a real food fat floor, and let carbs follow the training week. The [ISSN diets and body composition stand](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)<sup>[[G-Sources/S34-issn-diets-body-composition|S34]]</sup> covers that logic in detail.

And if fat loss is the goal, do not drop the bar. [Amely Verreijen](https://orcid.org/0000-0001-6327-7275) and colleagues studied adults 55 to 80 on a calorie cut. The people who combined higher protein with lifting were the ones who actually gained lean mass during the loss<sup>[[G-Sources/S12-verreijen-protein-weight-loss|S12]]</sup> ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-017-0229-6)). A diet that kills your strength work also kills your capacity. Keep [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]] in the fight.

Protein before bed helps too. [Trommelen](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl/j-trommelen) and [van Loon](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ljc-van-loon) show that a pre-sleep protein feeding can help overnight recovery when you are training<sup>[[G-Sources/S14-trommelen-presleep-protein|S14]]</sup>. ISSN talks about roughly 30 to 40 grams of a slower protein such as casein in that window<sup>[[G-Sources/S28-issn-protein-stand|S28]]</sup>. Count it toward your daily total. Then protect the night itself in [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]].

## Vitamins and minerals: the quiet part of the fuel system

Protein, carbs, and fat are the load in the tank. Just as important as the fuel your bodies needs is the additives that keeps your engine running clean. Vitamins and minerals help your body use that fuel: bone, blood, immunity, nerves, muscle, recovery. You need them whether you are cutting, maintaining, or building.

How do you get the micronutrients your body needs? Get them from food first. Build plates with vegetables and fruit, a real protein food, dairy or a fortified alternative when it fits you, and starches that match the day’s training. Your body needs a whole catalogue of nutrients every day to operate at full efficiency; the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements DRI tables](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx)<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup> list the vitamins and minerals and the recommended amounts by age and sex. That pattern covers the big groups most people need: calcium and vitamin D and magnesium for bone and muscle support; iron, B12, and folate for blood; vitamin C, zinc, and related nutrients for everyday resilience; sodium and potassium for fluid and nerve balance. When calories drop or training rises, gaps show up faster, which is why older-athlete reviews keep pointing at energy, protein, and nutrients like vitamin D<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup>.

You do not need to memorize every dose. By planning a balanced daily intake of the right kinds of foods, you will get the micro nutrients you need without having to measure every dose. Needs depend on age, sex, and life stage. For official numbers, go here:

- [NIH ODS Nutrient Recommendations and Databases](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx)<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup>
- [NIH ODS Vitamin and Mineral Fact Sheets](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-VitaminsMinerals/)
- [Dietary Guidelines: Food Sources of Select Nutrients](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/resources/2020-2025-dietary-guidelines-online-materials/food-sources-select-nutrients) — which foods supply calcium, potassium, fiber, vitamin D, and iron
- Quick starts: [Vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-Consumer/) · [Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-Consumer/) · [Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/) · [Magnesium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-Consumer/) · [Zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/) · [Vitamin B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/)
- Practical plate guide: Elevate Your Plate<sup>[[G-Sources/S29-body-signals-elevate-plate|S29]]</sup> (check claims against [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx) and [ISSN](https://www.issn.org), not a product pitch)

If you wonder about vitamin D, iron, or B12, get labs with your clinician. Guessing is how people buy the wrong bottle.

## Put the week together

None of this matters if the plan only works on a quiet Sunday. Name your goal. Estimate calories with the [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp) or the [USDA DRI Calculator](https://www.nal.usda.gov/human-nutrition-and-food-science/dri-calculator). Set protein from your body weight and raise it if you are cutting. Put more carbohydrate around hard training and less on easy days. Fill the plate with real food before powders. Buy the same groceries most weeks so the plan survives a real calendar.

Gadgets and supplement stacks are optional. Hitting protein, fueling sessions, and eating food that carries vitamins and minerals are not.

## Cautions: more is not always better

Too little hurts. Too much can hurt too.

Food rarely pushes most vitamins and minerals into the danger zone by itself. Trouble usually starts when people stack a multi, fortified foods, protein products, preworkout, and “immunity” megadoses on top of each other. Many nutrients have a safe upper limit. Look those limits up on [NIH ODS nutrient recommendations](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx)<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup> before you add another bottle. Vitamins A, D, E, and K can build up in the body. Iron, zinc, calcium, selenium, and high-dose magnesium from pills are common places fitness culture goes overboard; chronic high zinc can disturb copper, and iron you do not need can do real harm. Read the matching [ODS fact sheets](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-VitaminsMinerals/). Even a basic [multivitamin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/MVMS-Consumer/) is not automatically safe when it piles onto fortified food and other supplements.

Supplements are not drugs. Learn the basics on the [FDA Dietary Supplements page](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements), and check labels in the [NIH ODS Dietary Supplement Label Database](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/) when you need transparency.

Crash diets that wreck training, sleep, bone, or blood pressure are not discipline. They are a capacity problem<sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup>. Medications, kidney disease, gut issues, and age change what is safe. Do not self-prescribe high-dose iron, vitamin D, or fat-soluble megadoses. Ask a clinician.

If a podcast stack cannot survive a look at an [ODS fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-VitaminsMinerals/) and a [UL table](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx)<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup>, it does not belong in a long-term fuel plan.

## Decide and act

Decide what you want: lose fat, gain muscle, or maintain. Estimate your calories with the [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp) or the [USDA DRI Calculator](https://www.nal.usda.gov/human-nutrition-and-food-science/dri-calculator). Aim near 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram when you train to hold strength, and raise protein when you cut<sup>[[G-Sources/S28-issn-protein-stand|S28]]</sup> · <sup>[[G-Sources/S11-strasser-older-athletes|S11]]</sup> · <sup>[[G-Sources/S12-verreijen-protein-weight-loss|S12]]</sup>. Fuel hard days with carbohydrate, keep enough fat that the plan is livable<sup>[[G-Sources/S34-issn-diets-body-composition|S34]]</sup>, and cover vitamins and minerals with food before you chase pills<sup>[[G-Sources/S36-nih-ods-nutrient-recs|S36]]</sup>. Protect recovery in [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]], including overnight protein when it fits your digestion and schedule<sup>[[G-Sources/S14-trommelen-presleep-protein|S14]]</sup>.

I still want better evidence on how midlife changes in food processing show up as weight gain, fluid retention, and blood pressure in people who keep training. That pattern is not fully settled by the protein literature alone. [[A-Introduction/index|Introduction]] keeps that open list.

Food is fuel. Train with purpose in [[C-Exercise/index|Exercise]]. Recover on purpose in [[E-Sleep-and-recovery/index|Sleep and recovery]]. Then eat like someone who intends to stay in the fight.
